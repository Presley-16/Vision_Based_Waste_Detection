## Q1. What do peer-reviewed and institutional sources say about waste generation, collection coverage and illegal dumping in Zimbabwe, Harare, Bulawayo and SSA?

### Takeaway
SSA produces little waste per person (about 0.46 kg/cap/day in 2016) but collects only a small share of it. World Bank estimates of regional collection range from about 44% (What a Waste 2.0) down to 31% (current World Bank page), and UNEP reports that more than 90% of African waste ends up in uncontrolled dumpsites. Zimbabwean studies blame Harare's and Bulawayo's poor collection on population growth, infrequent collection, municipal incapacity and community schemes that have not worked.

## Q2. What evidence links uncollected waste and illegal dumping to cholera, typhoid, flooding and environmental harm?

### Takeaway
Zimbabwe's cholera epidemics are well documented: about 98,585 cases and 4,287 deaths in 2008–09, about 10,000 cases in 2018 centred on Glen View, Harare, and 34,550 cases with 719 deaths in 2023–24. Official reports, however, blame contaminated water and sanitation failure first. Uncollected waste is linked to these outbreaks mainly through reviews and conceptual frameworks, not attributional studies.

### Cited Findings
13. **Mukandavire, Z., Liao, S., Wang, J., Gaff, H., Smith, D.L. and Morris, J.G. (2011)** 'Estimating the reproductive numbers for the 2008–2009 cholera outbreaks in Zimbabwe', *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 108(21), pp. 8767–8772. doi:10.1073/pnas.1019712108. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: the 2008–09 epidemic caused **98,585 reported cases and 4,287 deaths**. Estimated basic reproductive numbers varied widely by province, from just above 1 to **2.72**.
14. **Chigudu, S. (2019)** 'The politics of cholera, crisis and citizenship in urban Zimbabwe: "People were dying like flies"', *African Affairs*, 118(472), pp. 413–434. doi:10.1093/afraf/ady068. **[Crossref ✓]** (Note: the DOI is **ady068**, not ady064.) Key findings: the 2008/09 outbreak caused "an unprecedented 100,000 cases and nearly 5,000 deaths". Residents of Harare's high-density townships saw it as a "man-made" disaster rooted in the state's failure to provide services.
15. **WHO (2018)** *Cholera – Zimbabwe*. Disease Outbreak News, 5 October 2018. Available at: https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/05-october-2018-cholera-zimbabwe-en. **[Web ✓]** Key findings: by 3 Oct 2018 there were **8,535 cases (163 lab-confirmed) and 50 deaths (CFR 0.6%)**, 98% of them in Harare. **Glen View** (an informal trading hub) was the epicentre, alongside Budiriro. The suspected source was contaminated wells and boreholes, on which about 69% of Harare's population relies. A state of disaster was declared on 12 Sept 2018. *This DON does **not** mention uncollected garbage or sewer bursts.* Earlier DON (20 Sept 2018): 3,621 cases and 32 deaths ([WHO](https://www.who.int/emergencies/disease-outbreak-news/item/20-september-2018-cholera-zimbabwe-en)).
16. **UNICEF (2025)** *Zimbabwe Humanitarian Situation Report No. 9 (End-Year), January–December 2024*. Available at: https://reliefweb.int/report/zimbabwe/unicef-zimbabwe-humanitarian-situation-report-no-9-end-year-january-december-2024. **[Web ✓ via search]** Key findings: the 2023–24 outbreak ended in June 2024 after **34,550 cases and 719 deaths (CFR 2.1%)**. A new flare-up in November 2024 had produced 228 cases and 2 deaths by 31 Dec 2024. WHO AFRO's close-out note gives **718 deaths** ([WHO AFRO](https://afro.who.int/countries/zimbabwe/news/zimbabwe-declares-cholera-outbreak-over)), a minor discrepancy caused by different cut-off dates.

The authors note that the epidemiological evidence is thin and mostly indirect *(verify wording)*.
18. **Lamond, J., Bhattacharya, N. and Bloch, R. (2012)** 'The role of solid waste management as a response to urban flood risk in developing countries, a case study analysis', *WIT Transactions on Ecology and the Environment*, 159, pp. 193–204. doi:10.2495/FRIAR120161. **[Crossref ✓]** (Crossref lists vol. "1"; the WIT volume 159 should be checked.) Key findings: case studies showing that solid waste blocking drains makes urban flooding in developing-country cities worse, and that better SWM is an inexpensive flood-risk measure. Pre-2015 but seminal on this link.

### Inferences
- In the dissertation, the safer wording is that uncollected waste and illegal dumping are **risk factors that co-occur with** the failures of water and sanitation infrastructure behind the cholera outbreaks. Do not claim that dumping "caused" the 2018 or 2023–24 outbreaks: WHO attributes 2018 to contaminated groundwater sources.

### Gaps
- I found no peer-reviewed study that statistically attributes Harare cholera or typhoid cases to proximity to illegal dumpsites.
- I found no Zimbabwe-specific peer-reviewed study that quantifies flooding caused by blocked drains (in Harare or Bulawayo). The evidence is international (e.g., Accra, Lagos) or comes from press reports.

## Q3. What is known about municipal capacity (vehicles, budget, collection frequency) in Harare and Bulawayo?

### Takeaway
Both councils have chronically short and poorly maintained refuse fleets. Harare has needed roughly 40 compactors but at times had only about 15. Bulawayo had about 10 against a need of 26. In 2025 Harare's collection was partly handed to a private concessionaire (Geo Pomona). All the figures come from press reports, not peer-reviewed sources.

### Cited Findings (grey literature; cite cautiously)
- Harare is reported to generate close to 1,000 t/day while its fleet (15 compactors, 7 tippers, 3 skip trucks) can collect about 360 t/day. Undated Herald report — [Herald: "Garbage collection overwhelms council"](https://www.heraldonline.co.zw/garbage-collection-overwhelms-council/).
- A later report counts 48 compactors and 4 skip trucks, with only about 50% operational because of spare-parts shortages. The fleet figures contradict each other across years — [Herald](https://www.heraldonline.co.zw/harare-chi-town-choke-on-own-garbage/).
- In 2016 Harare paid US$3.1m for 15 compactors that were never delivered — [Nehanda Radio/Herald 2018](https://nehandaradio.com/2018/06/13/rbz-gives-city-600k-to-import-refuse-compactors/).
- In 2025 Geo Pomona took over collection with 45 trucks and 100 skip bins and is expected to collect about 650 t/day. This was contested by residents' associations — [AllAfrica 2025](https://allafrica.com/stories/202506060002.html).
- Bulawayo had 10 compactors against a requirement of 26 (2021) — [NewsDay via PressReader, 7 Jun 2021](https://www.pressreader.com/zimbabwe/newsday-zimbabwe/20210607/281603833399356). BCC identified **35 illegal dumpsites in the CBD**, which it linked to fleet breakdowns — [The Independent/NewsDay](https://theindependent.co.zw/local-news/article/200000414/bcc-identifies-35-illegal-dumpsites-in-cbd).
- Kwenda et al. (2022) [entry 8] supports "low collection frequency" and "municipal incapacitation" as the main causes in the peer-reviewed literature.

### Inferences
- The capacity gap (collection ability of roughly a third of what Harare generates) gives a quantitative case for prioritising collection by reported hotspot rather than by fixed schedule.

### Gaps
- Council budgets for refuse services and official collection-frequency schedules were not found in any verifiable primary source (budget statements and council minutes). No 2026 figures were found.

## Q4. What citizen-reporting and participatory-sensing apps exist for waste and civic problems, and how have they been evaluated?

### Takeaway
Waste-specific apps such as Marine Debris Tracker and OpenLitterMap are described in peer-reviewed venues mainly as systems papers. The strongest outcome evidence comes from civic-reporting platforms: on FixMyStreet, citizens who get a response to their first report are 57% more likely to report again. 311 studies show that participation is uneven across neighbourhoods. Vision-based garbage detection from smartphones dates back at least to SpotGarbage (2016).

### Cited Findings
19. **Jambeck, J.R. and Johnsen, K. (2015)** 'Citizen-based litter and marine debris data collection and mapping', *Computing in Science & Engineering*, 17(4), pp. 20–26. doi:10.1109/MCSE.2015.67. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: describes the design and use of the **Marine Debris Tracker** mobile app, which records geolocated litter items for open mapping. No abstract on Crossref; usage statistics not extracted *(verify)*.
20. **Lynch, S. (2018)** 'OpenLitterMap.com – open data on plastic pollution with blockchain rewards (Littercoin)', *Open Geospatial Data, Software and Standards*, 3, 6. doi:10.1186/s40965-018-0050-y. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: presents **OpenLitterMap**, a platform for crowdsourcing geotagged litter photos as open data, with a cryptocurrency incentive (Littercoin) to keep people participating. It depends on smartphone photo geotags, as the dissertation's system does.
21. **Mittal, G., Yagnik, K.B., Garg, M. and Krishnan, N.C. (2016)** 'SpotGarbage: smartphone app to detect garbage using deep learning', in *Proceedings of the 2016 ACM International Joint Conference on Pervasive and Ubiquitous Computing (UbiComp '16)*. New York: ACM, pp. 940–945. doi:10.1145/2971648.2971731. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: an early smartphone app that uses a CNN to detect garbage in geotagged images and support civic reporting in India. The accuracy figure (often cited as ~87.7% on the GINI dataset) was not re-checked *(verify)*. A direct precursor to the dissertation.
22. **King, S.F. and Brown, P. (2007)** 'Fix my street or else: using the internet to voice local public service concerns', in *Proceedings of the 1st International Conference on Theory and Practice of Electronic Governance (ICEGOV '07)*. New York: ACM, pp. 72–80. doi:10.1145/1328057.1328076. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: an early evaluation of the UK **FixMyStreet** site as a channel for reporting local problems (fly-tipping, potholes) to councils, and its role in public accountability. Pre-2015, seminal.
23. **Sjoberg, F.M., Mellon, J. and Peixoto, T. (2017)** 'The effect of bureaucratic responsiveness on citizen participation', *Public Administration Review*, 77(3), pp. 340–351. doi:10.1111/puar.12697. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: using **399,364** FixMyStreet users, a successful first report was associated with a **57% higher probability** of submitting a second one. Responsiveness to the first report predicted all later reporting. In short, people keep using these systems only if the city closes the loop.
24. **Minkoff, S.L. (2016)** 'NYC 311: a tract-level analysis of citizen–government contacting in New York City', *Urban Affairs Review*, 52(2), pp. 211–246. doi:10.1177/1078087415577796. **[Crossref ✓]** (online 2015.) Key findings: a tract-level analysis showing that 311 contact rates vary with neighbourhood socio-economic and demographic characteristics. Crowdsourced reports therefore measure who reports, not only where problems are *(verify specific coefficients)*.

### Inferences
- The evaluated outcome that matters most for the dissertation is the **feedback loop** (Sjoberg et al.). A Zimbabwean reporting app should show users the council's status updates, or participation will drop.
- Reporting bias (Minkoff) means hotspot maps built from reports alone will under-represent low-engagement areas. This is an argument for combining reports with systematic detection.

### Gaps
- **Litterati, TrashOut and SeeClickFix**: I found no peer-reviewed evaluation of these apps that could be verified (Crossref searches returned nothing relevant). Cite their websites only as system descriptions, or omit them.
- I found **no peer-reviewed study of a waste-reporting app deployed in Zimbabwe or SSA** (e.g., Kenyan, Nigerian or South African apps). This is a clear research gap the dissertation can claim.

## Q5. What does the literature say about the quality of volunteered geographic information (VGI)?

### Takeaway
Goodchild (2007) framed citizens as sensors. Follow-up work shows that VGI can approach authoritative accuracy (Haklay, 2010), but quality varies in space and over time. Several assurance approaches have been proposed: crowdsourcing, social and geographic checks (Goodchild and Li, 2012), credibility frameworks, and the methods catalogued by Senaratne et al. (2017). The citizen-science literature (Kosmala et al., 2016) adds that volunteer data can match expert data if projects build in validation.

### Cited Findings
25. **Goodchild, M.F. (2007)** 'Citizens as sensors: the world of volunteered geography', *GeoJournal*, 69(4), pp. 211–221. doi:10.1007/s10708-007-9111-y. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: coins "volunteered geographic information" and argues that GPS-equipped citizens act as a network of sensors, while raising questions of accuracy, motivation and privacy. The seminal paper.
26. **Flanagin, A.J. and Metzger, M.J. (2008)** 'The credibility of volunteered geographic information', *GeoJournal*, 72(3–4), pp. 137–148. doi:10.1007/s10708-008-9188-y. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: frames VGI quality as a matter of credibility, involving source expertise, the shift of responsibility from producers to users, and the need for users to judge reliability.
27. **Haklay, M. (2010)** 'How good is volunteered geographical information? A comparative study of OpenStreetMap and Ordnance Survey datasets', *Environment and Planning B: Planning and Design*, 37(4), pp. 682–703. doi:10.1068/b35097. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: OSM positional accuracy in London was comparable to Ordnance Survey data (about 80% overlap of motorway features within about 6 m), but coverage was uneven and poorer in deprived areas *(verify exact figures)*.
28. **Goodchild, M.F. and Li, L. (2012)** 'Assuring the quality of volunteered geographic information', *Spatial Statistics*, 1, pp. 110–120. doi:10.1016/j.spasta.2012.03.002. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: proposes three quality-assurance approaches for VGI: **crowdsourcing** (peer correction), **social** (trusted gatekeepers or moderators) and **geographic** (checking against geographic knowledge and rules).
29. **Senaratne, H., Mobasheri, A., Ali, A.L., Capineri, C. and Haklay, M. (2017)** 'A review of volunteered geographic information quality assessment methods', *International Journal of Geographical Information Science*, 31(1), pp. 139–167. doi:10.1080/13658816.2016.1189556. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: a systematic review that sorts VGI quality-assessment methods by data type (map-based, image-based and text-based VGI) and by quality element (positional and thematic accuracy, completeness, credibility). The image-based VGI category is the one relevant to geotagged waste photos.
30. **Kosmala, M., Wiggins, A., Swanson, A. and Simmons, B. (2016)** 'Assessing data quality in citizen science', *Frontiers in Ecology and the Environment*, 14(10), pp. 551–560. doi:10.1002/fee.1436. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: many citizen-science projects produce data as accurate as professionals' or better, provided they use training, expert validation, replication and statistical correction for bias. Each dataset should be judged on its own design.
- Supplementary (verified, optional): **Spielman, S.E. (2014)** 'Spatial collective intelligence? Credibility, accuracy, and volunteered geographic information', *Cartography and Geographic Information Science*, 41(2), pp. 115–124. doi:10.1080/15230406.2013.874200. **[Crossref ✓]**

### Inferences
- An automated YOLOv11 check on submitted photos can serve as a machine form of Goodchild and Li's geographic and social quality assurance, filtering out spam and non-waste images before they reach council staff. It also matches Kosmala et al.'s recommendation to build in validation.

### Gaps
- I found no published VGI-quality study specific to waste reports from African cities, or to the reliability of smartphone EXIF GPS in Zimbabwe.

## Q6. What is known about privacy in street-level and crowdsourced imagery, and about Zimbabwe's data protection law?

### Takeaway
Blurring faces and licence plates automatically at scale has been established practice since Google Street View (Frome et al., 2009). In Zimbabwe, the Cyber and Data Protection Act [Chapter 12:07] (Act 5 of 2021, in force in corrected form from 11 March 2022) makes **POTRAZ** the Data Protection Authority (s.5). Since 13 Sept 2024, **SI 155 of 2024** has required data controllers to be licensed and to appoint a DPO, and POTRAZ inspections are announced to begin on 1 Sept 2026.

### Cited Findings
31. **Frome, A., Cheung, G., Abdulkader, A., Zennaro, M., Wu, B., Bissacco, A., Adam, H., Neven, H. and Vincent, L. (2009)** 'Large-scale privacy protection in Google Street View', in *2009 IEEE 12th International Conference on Computer Vision (ICCV)*. Kyoto: IEEE, pp. 2373–2380. doi:10.1109/ICCV.2009.5459413. **[Crossref ✓]** (first six authors confirmed on Crossref.) Key findings: describes Google's automatic system for detecting and blurring faces and licence plates in Street View imagery. It reported about 89% face recall and 94–96% plate recall, with blurring tuned to favour recall over precision *(verify exact figures in the paper)*.
32. **Government of Zimbabwe (2021)** *Cyber and Data Protection Act [Chapter 12:07]*, Act No. 5 of 2021 (republished with corrected title, GN 492/2022, 11 March 2022). Harare: Government Printer. Available at: https://www.veritaszim.net/sites/veritas_d/files/Cyber%20%26%20Data%20Protection%20Act%20Cap1207%20No%205%20of%202021%20gaz%202022-03-11.pdf and https://zimlii.org/akn/zw/act/2021/5. **[Web ✓]** Key findings: s.5 makes POTRAZ, established under the Postal and Telecommunications Act [Chapter 12:05], the Data Protection Authority. *Citation note:* Veritas also hosts an earlier mis-titled version ("Data Protection Act", Cap 11:22), so cite the March 2022 republication. Specific section numbers for the data-quality, purpose-limitation, sensitive or biometric data, security, breach and transborder-transfer provisions **were not read in this session** because ZimLII returned 403. Check them against the Veritas PDF before citing.
33. **Government of Zimbabwe (2024)** *Statutory Instrument 155 of 2024: Cyber and Data Protection (Licensing of Data Controllers and Appointment of Data Protection Officers) Regulations, 2024*. Harare: Government Printer, 13 September 2024. Available at: https://techzim.co.zw/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/S.I.-155-of-2024.-Cyber-and-Data-Protection-13sept2024.pdf. **[Web ✓ exists; text not read]** Key findings *(secondary)*: made under s.32 of the Act. It introduces data controller licensing (commonly reported compliance deadline: 12 March 2025) and mandatory DPOs. Secondary sources disagree on the licensing threshold (50+ data subjects vs. all commercial processing) and on the DPO deadline, and one guide reports a **24-hour breach notification** to POTRAZ ([MISA Zimbabwe](https://zimbabwe.misa.org/2024/11/09/misa-zimbabwe-analysis-and-position-on-new-data-regulations); [Afriwise](https://www.afriwise.com/blog/a-quick-start-guide-to-zimbabwes-data-protection-regulations)). POTRAZ Regulatory Notice 2 of 2026 announces compliance inspections from **1 Sept 2026** ([MEA TechWatch, Mar 2026](https://meatechwatch.com/2026/03/25/data-crackdown-looms-potraz-targets-unlicensed-data-handlers/)).
34. **Mutiro, B. and Saki, O. (2024)** 'The Cyber and Data Protection Act of Zimbabwe: a critical analysis', *African Journal on Privacy and Data Protection*, 1(1). doi:10.29053/ajpdp.v1i1.0004. **[Crossref ✓]** Key findings: a peer-reviewed legal analysis of the Act. No abstract on Crossref, so the specific critiques were not extracted *(verify)*. Civil society (MISA) separately raises concerns about POTRAZ's independence and possible political influence over licensing.

### Inferences
- A system that collects geotagged photos from citizens processes personal data: the reporter's location and identity, and any faces or plates of bystanders. Under the Act it would probably be a "data controller" (the council or operator) needing an SI 155 licence and a DPO. Automatically blurring faces and plates before storage, in the manner of Frome et al. (2009), and stripping or coarsening EXIF data are defensible privacy-by-design measures.

### Gaps
- The exact wording of the Act's provisions on sensitive or biometric data and transborder transfers, and the gazetted SI 155 licensing threshold, were not read from primary text.
- I found no Zimbabwean case law or POTRAZ guidance specific to images, CCTV or crowdsourced photos.

## Synthesis notes: agreements, contradictions and gaps

**Agreements**
- All institutional sources (WaW 2.0, AWMO 2018, the current World Bank page) agree that SSA has low per-capita generation, low collection, and dominant open dumping and burning. Zimbabwean case studies (Kwenda et al., 2022; Sinthumule and Mkumbuzi, 2019) agree that municipal incapacity and low collection frequency are the main drivers.
- Health sources agree on the scale of the cholera outbreaks: about 98.6k cases and 4.3k deaths in 2008–09 (Mukandavire et al.; Chigudu says "100,000 / nearly 5,000"), about 10k in 2018, and 34.5k cases with 718–719 deaths in 2023–24.
- The VGI and citizen-science literature agrees that crowdsourced data can be good enough to use if validation is designed in.

**Contradictions or inconsistencies to handle explicitly**
- SSA collection coverage: **44%** (WaW 2.0, 2016 data) vs **31%** (current World Bank page, 2022 data) vs about **55%** (AWMO, Africa-wide, c.2012). These differ in vintage, geography and method, so state which one you use.
- Harare generation: about **762 t/day** (UN-Habitat WaCT) vs "close to **1,000 t/day**" (Herald). Harare fleet: 15 compactors vs 48 compactors (about 50% operational) in different years.
- 2023–24 cholera deaths: 719 (UNICEF) vs 718 (WHO AFRO). The origin district is also reported differently (Chegutu vs Buhera).
- **Cause attribution:** WHO (2018) attributes the Harare outbreak to contaminated wells and boreholes and failing water supply, not to waste. Local media and some reviews blame uncollected garbage. The dissertation should present waste as a co-located environmental risk factor, not a proven cause.
- Chigudu (2019) DOI: commonly mis-cited; the correct DOI is 10.1093/afraf/ady068.

**Gaps (research opportunities the dissertation can claim)**
1. No verifiable official (ZIMSTAT or EMA) time series on waste generation, collection coverage or illegal dumpsite counts for Harare or Bulawayo.
2. No peer-reviewed evaluation of any waste-reporting app in Zimbabwe or SSA, and none of Litterati, TrashOut or SeeClickFix specifically.
3. No study spatially linking illegal dumpsites to cholera or typhoid incidence or to drain-blockage flooding in Zimbabwean cities.
4. No VGI-quality study of image-based waste reports in African contexts.
5. No Zimbabwe-specific guidance on privacy in crowdsourced images under the Cyber and Data Protection Act and SI 155.
