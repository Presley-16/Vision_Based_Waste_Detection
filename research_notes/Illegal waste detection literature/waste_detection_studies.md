## Key Question 1: Public datasets (sizes, classes, licences, image domain)

### Takeaway
The public waste datasets split into four image domains: (a) single objects on clean backgrounds (TrashNet, WaDaBa, Drinking Waste, MJU-Waste); (b) "in-the-wild" ground-level litter photos (TACO, PlastOPol, OpenLitterMap, GINI, pLitterStreet, RoLID-11K); (c) industrial conveyor scenes (ZeroWaste); and (d) aerial/UAV imagery (UAVVaste, AerialWaste, DroneWaste). None of the ground-level street datasets found here comes from sub-Saharan Africa. Most are mainly European or Asian. Most are object-centric, labelling individual items, rather than labelling **piles/dumps** as a single "waste" region the way the Zimbabwe dataset does.

### Inferences
- The Zimbabwe dataset (single "waste" class over **piles**, smartphone, sub-Saharan Africa, with negatives) fills a real gap. The closest public analogues are GINI (piles, phone/web images, India) and PlastOPol/UAVVaste (single class but item-level).
- Item-level datasets (TACO, PlastOPol) give each bottle or wrapper its own box. Dump-pile datasets have no clear object boundaries. This makes box-level mAP on piles inherently noisier: the same pile can validly be labelled as one box or several.
- Datasets differ in licence. ZeroWaste is non-commercial (CC BY-NC); TACO/TrashNet are MIT; PlastOPol is CC BY; GINI and Drinking Waste are CC0. This matters if the dissertation reuses any of them for pre-training.

### Gaps
- TACO's arXiv ID and TrashNet's report details were not re-fetched; they come from prior knowledge but are consistent with the catalogue.
- Licence and exact image counts for OpenLitterMap, plus country and size for pLitterStreet and StreetView-Waste, were not stated on the pages fetched.
- Whether TACO, PlastOPol or UAVVaste include deliberately curated negative images was not confirmed from the original papers.

## Key Question 2: Detection and classification studies (YOLO and others), including Africa and mobile deployments

### Takeaway
Reported performance ranges from about 95–99% (classification on clean-background or single-object datasets, or narrow tasks such as "is this bin full") down to about 40–58% mAP@0.5 for multi-object detection in cluttered real-world scenes (pLitterStreet AP above 40%; EcoDetect-YOLO 58.1% mAP@0.5; Majchrowska up to about 70% AP). No peer-reviewed ground-level (smartphone/street) waste-detection study from Zimbabwe, Nigeria, Kenya, Ghana, Rwanda or South Africa was found. The only sub-Saharan-Africa deep-learning waste-detection study located uses **crowdsourced UAV imagery** (Knoblauch et al., 10 countries). Very few studies test on images from a different country or domain than they trained on.

### Inferences
- Headline figures of 94–99% (Nasien, Abo-Zahhad, Alharbi, YOLO-MTG, TrashNet classifiers) come from classification, narrow container tasks, or authors' own constrained datasets. Box-level mAP on uncontrolled outdoor scenes clusters around 40–70% (pLitterStreet, EcoDetect, Majchrowska). The dissertation's box mAP@0.5 of about 0.40 sits at the low end of this range, which is expected for amorphous dump piles in Zimbabwean scenes. Its about 93% photo-level accuracy is comparable to image- or tile-level figures (SpotGarbage 87.69%; Knoblauch 92.87%).
- Rajeev et al. give direct evidence of domain sensitivity: 0.821 drops to 0.437 mAP@0.5 on another dataset with the same YOLOv8.
- Cross-country evaluation is rare. Only Moral et al. (11 Spanish cities) and Knoblauch et al. (29 SSA regions) evaluate across multiple locations. None found trains on European or Asian street litter and tests in Africa.
- Negative images are explicitly part of image-level or act-level tasks (SpotGarbage, Alharbi, Moral) but are rarely reported in detection-only papers.

## Key Question 3: Typical mAP ranges for cluttered litter detection, and why they are low

### Takeaway
On TACO and similar in-the-wild data, multi-class mAP@0.5 is often low: about 0.12 to 0.41 in documented YOLOv5/YOLOv8 student replications. Single-class or curated datasets reach about 58–88%. Authors attribute the gap to small objects, long-tail class imbalance, cluttered backgrounds, class confusion between visually similar materials, dim or occluded targets, and ambiguous category definitions.

### Cited Findings
- YOLOv5s on TACO, in a documented course project: mAP@0.5 ≈ 0.124 (P ≈ 0.11, R ≈ 0.17) — [GitHub e96031413](https://github.com/e96031413/TACO-Trash-Detection-YOLOv5). This is a non-peer-reviewed baseline.
- YOLOv8n on TACO: mAP@0.5 = 0.409, mAP@0.5:0.95 = 0.303, after dropping ambiguous classes "Unlabeled litter" and "Other plastic" because they "do not represent clearly defined object classes" — [GitHub ZihanYuan0111](https://github.com/ZihanYuan0111/ENGG2112-TACO-Object-Detection). Non-peer-reviewed.
- A search summary claimed the original TACO paper reported YOLOv5 92.96% / YOLOv8 97.63% mAP. This is **implausible and rejected**: TACO (2020) predates YOLOv8 (2023), so the figures belong to some unrelated paper.
- pLitterStreet: AP above 40% for all four detectors on more than 13,000 street images — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2401.14719).
- EcoDetect-YOLO: mAP@0.5 58.1%, mAP@0.5:0.95 31.1%. Paper class about 45%. Failure modes: similar-coloured materials, dim, blurry and partially visible targets — [PMC](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11280945/).
- Majchrowska et al.: up to about 70% AP for single-class "litter" detection in the wild — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2105.06808).
- RoLID-11K: "pronounced long-tail and small-object distributions". Litter is very small and sparse against cluttered verges; real-time YOLOs are limited by "coarse feature hierarchies" — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/abs/2601.00398).
- ZeroWaste frames the core difficulty as "deformable object segmentation in cluttered scenes" — [Crossref title, CVPR 2022](https://doi.org/10.1109/CVPR52688.2022.02047).
- YOLO-MTG authors: existing datasets rarely include "densely accumulated" multi-category garbage — [Springer](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11760-024-03220-2).
- Fotovvatikhah et al.: dataset imbalance, real-world variability and lack of standardisation are key limitations — [ResearchGate](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/391865818_A_Systematic_Review_of_AI-Based_Techniques_for_Automated_Waste_Classification).
- A 2026 UAV litter review notes YOLOv8 at 71.3% vs Faster R-CNN at 31.7% mAP@0.5 at 30 m altitude in one study, and cites dataset variability and limited standardised benchmarks — [Springer MTAP review](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s11042-026-21440-1) (secondary summary).

### Inferences
- For **piles** (the Zimbabwe use case), box-level IoU is ill-posed: annotators may draw one box around a whole dump or several around sub-piles. A prediction that covers the pile "correctly" can still fail the IoU ≥ 0.5 test. This explains low box mAP alongside high photo-level ("is there waste?") accuracy, and justifies reporting image-level accuracy, precision and recall, including on negative images, as the operational metric. This is my inference; none of the sources tests it on dump piles directly.
- The comparison is only fair when class taxonomy (1 vs 10 vs 60 classes), split, image size and the mAP definition match. Many papers do not report these consistently.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed paper was found that publishes a standard TACO-60 benchmark table for YOLOv8/YOLO11. Peer-reviewed TACO numbers (e.g., Córdova 2022's TACO results) were not retrieved.
- No source was found that quantifies annotation inconsistency (inter-annotator agreement) for waste piles.
